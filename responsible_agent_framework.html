Responsible agent deployment and evaluation

A draft framework for customer-facing AI agents (support and sales): how to tier risk, what to require before launch, how to score agent responses, and how to calibrate an LLM judge well enough to trust it.

Version 0.4 (draft)6 October 2026Follows: MLOps reference architecture
Status: proposed, not measured. This is a design framework. Nothing in it was tested on a live deployment. Each element is tagged to show its basis: Sourced backed by a published source checked for this draft, Practice consistent with published practice but not itself a measured result, and Starting value a proposed number to be tuned against the deployment’s own data. Agreement with published standards and vendor guidance (Alignment tab) shows the structure is consistent with accepted practice. It is not evidence that any threshold works.

Two halves, one principle

The framework has a deployment half (risk tiering, launch gates, operational controls, governance) and an evaluation half (a rubric scoring task success, faithfulness and instruction-following, judged by an LLM that is itself calibrated against human ratings). The two are meant to be implemented together, because launch gates depend on evaluation thresholds and evaluation depends on the traces the controls produce.

The guiding principle is graduated autonomy: an agent earns the right to act more independently as evidence of reliability accumulates. Defaults are conservative, and expansion happens through measurement rather than assumption.

Scope

In scope are support triage, FAQ answering, account-status questions, troubleshooting, lead qualification, product recommendation, scheduling and light transactional flows such as a refund within policy limits. Out of scope are agents that negotiate contracts, give regulated medical, legal or financial advice, or operate in safety-critical domains; these need additional controls.

Changes from the first draft. The customer-facing definition was reworded to remove a conflict with the human-in-the-loop requirement. The judge may now answer “insufficient evidence” instead of forcing a score. A stopping condition (turn and tool-call budget) was added to tool design. The calibration section now states how many severe-failure examples are needed for its recall target to be demonstrable. Threshold numbers are labelled as starting values, and the EU dates were updated. In version 0.3, a review against NIST, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, FINOS and OWASP material added repeated-trial reliability (pass^k), review of passed traces, an attack-success-rate metric and a gaps list (Alignment tab). Version 0.4 adds requirement-level corroboration from Microsoft’s Responsible AI Standard v2 and its 2026 Transparency Report: an impact assessment and inventory entry, an oversight test, per-group quality of service, a customer failure-reporting channel, measured time to switch off and roll back, an agent card, phased rollout, and added attack classes (permission escalation, memory poisoning). It also adds two cautions from the same report: judge and red-teaming metrics have documented validity gaps, and some models behave differently when they detect evaluation.

Tiers and the launch checklist

Each deployment is placed in one tier, and the tier determines which controls are mandatory. Select a tier to filter the checklist and see its thresholds. Promoting an agent to a higher tier means re-running the checklist for the new tier and meeting its evaluation thresholds. Most incidents are expected shortly after scope expands, so promotion is treated as a new deployment.

Operational controls and governance

Human-in-the-loop triggers

Human oversight is the safety net, not an option. Handoff is triggered by any of the following, and the handoff is itself measured (time to human and post-handoff resolution).

TriggerConditionTier
ConfidenceThe agent expresses uncertainty above a set threshold.All
TopicAny intent on the deny-list or escalation list.All
SentimentDetected distress or repeated frustration.2 and 3
ActionAny irreversible or high-value action.3
Customer requestAny request for a person, honoured immediately and without friction.All

Practice Published agent guidance recommends pausing for human feedback at checkpoints or on blockers, sandbox testing before production, and stopping conditions such as an iteration limit.

Logging and monitoring

Every trace records timestamp, session ID, customer ID or anonymised token, prompt version, model and version, retrieved documents (IDs and content hashes), tool calls with inputs and outputs, the final response, any guardrail trips, and the eventual outcome (resolved, escalated, abandoned). Logs feed real-time monitoring, the evaluation pipeline and audit.

Minimum dashboard: volume and intent mix, resolution and handoff rates, refusal rate (categorised), tool success and error rates, customer-visible errors, latency (p50 and p95), and cost per resolved conversation. On-call is paged when refusal, handoff or error rate leaves its baseline by a defined margin within a rolling window. Starting value The margin and window are deployment-specific.

Incident response and kill switch

Severity levels follow the organisation’s existing incident framework. AI-specific Sev-1 examples are an unauthorised commitment, a PII leak, dangerous instructions, and a systemic hallucination spike. A single operator action must take the agent out of customer-facing rotation and route all traffic to humans, and it is tested at least quarterly. Starting value The quarterly cadence.

Change management and review cadence

A versioned change with an evaluation re-run is required before production for any of: a system prompt edit, a model version or vendor change, a new or changed tool spec, a retrieval pipeline change, or ingestion of a new knowledge-base source. Hot fixes take an expedited path but still leave an audit record. Reviews run weekly (operations), monthly (evaluation and human spot-checks), quarterly (charter) and annually (full audit and red-team exercise). Starting value The cadence.

Disclosure and user rights

Customers are told at the start of a session that they are interacting with an AI, a path to a human is always available and is surfaced after any failure, and customers can request their transcript where privacy law provides for it. Sourced For the EU, Article 50(1) of the AI Act requires systems that interact directly with people to be designed so users are informed they are dealing with an AI, at the latest at first interaction, in a clear and distinguishable way, unless this is obvious to a reasonably well-informed person. It applies from 2 August 2026.

This is a regulatory summary, not legal advice. Whether an agent is also “high-risk” under the Act depends on its use case; the omnibus amendment moved stand-alone high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 and embedded-product obligations to 2 August 2028, and left the Article 50 date unchanged.

Rubric

Three dimensions are scored independently on a 0 to 4 scale. No composite score is computed. A composite lets one strong dimension mask a critical failure in another, so each dimension has its own pass rule. Thresholds are starting values and are shown for the tier selected on the Tiers tab.

The average and the tail rule do different jobs. An average can look healthy while a few severe failures sit in the tail, so the tail rule is the binding constraint for safety and the average tracks general quality. Averaging ordinal scores is a convenience, not a measurement-theory claim.

Why 0 to 4

Practice A five-point scale with a midpoint invites the safe middle. A 0 to 4 scale reserves 0 for severe failure and removes the soft midpoint. Whether judges actually use the full range is checked in calibration rather than assumed.

Not covered in this version

Tone, brand voice, empathy and cost are not scored. Adding them now would dilute calibration of the core three. Tone is easier to spot-check by humans, and cost is observable directly without a judge.

The judge

The judge is a structured scoring program implemented as an LLM call. It scales rough but consistent scoring across large volumes of traces. Its outputs feed dashboards, regression gates and human triage; they are not final verdicts on high-stakes decisions.

Design choices

  • One call per dimension. Sourced Published agent-evaluation guidance recommends separate judges for separate dimensions, to stop the model conflating them (“it was helpful, so it is probably faithful”). The cost is three calls per trace, mitigated by sampling.
  • Allow “insufficient evidence”. Sourced Added The same guidance recommends giving the judge an explicit way out. A null score is routed to human review instead of being forced into 0 to 4.
  • Different model family, locked version. Sourced Self-preference is documented (see bias table), so the judge should come from a different family than the agent where possible. A judge upgrade counts as a change that requires recalibration.
  • Judge capability. Practice The judge should be at least as capable as the agent under test.
  • Excerpt selection is a bias source. Added The prompt includes “relevant excerpts” of the system prompt. Whoever or whatever selects them shapes the score, so selection is rule-based and versioned, or the full prompt is used.

Calibration

  
Calibration turns a plausible-looking judge into a measurement instrument. A gold set is built from 200 to 500 traces stratified across intents, outcomes and tiers, scored by two trained human raters working blind, with a third rater or documented rule to resolve disagreements. It is a living asset, refreshed quarterly. Starting value The set size.

  
Agreement measures

  
    
    
  • Quadratic weighted kappa per dimension, which penalises large disagreements more than small ones and suits ordinal scores.
    • 
    
  • Spearman correlation between judge and human consensus.
    • 
    
  • Confusion matrix, especially around the pass/fail boundary.
    • 
    
  • Recall on score-0 cases, because missing a severe failure is the most expensive judge error.
    • 
  

  
Sourced For context, in the benchmark study above, GPT-4 agreed with human experts 85% of the time against 81% agreement between humans, on one pairwise task with 2023 models. That shows agreement near human-human level is attainable in some settings, not that any given judge reaches it.

  
Sourced Added Two cautions from Microsoft’s 2026 Transparency Report (a vendor self-report). First, its research group reports significant validity gaps in commonly used LLM-as-judge evaluations, and its remedy is a framework that defines how an issue is measured and whether the measure is valid, which is what the calibration step here is meant to establish. Second, it reports that reasoning-focused models can change behaviour when they detect they are being evaluated rather than deployed, and were more likely to comply with harmful requests framed as hypothetical or consequence-free. Offline results may therefore not transfer to production, which is why production sampling is the only test that reflects real behaviour.

  
Acceptance thresholds Starting value

  
Before the judge may gate any decision: quadratic weighted kappa ≥ 0.7 per dimension, recall on score-0 cases ≥ 0.95 per dimension, and no systematic over- or under-scoring across intents. The 0.7 figure sits in the “substantial” band (0.61 to 0.80) of the widely used Landis and Koch labels, which are themselves conventions rather than derived cut-offs.


  
How many severe examples are enough?

  
The recall target is only demonstrable with enough true score-0 cases. A 200 to 500 trace gold set drawn from ordinary traffic may hold a handful of them, which cannot support a 0.95 claim. The calculator gives the one-sided 95% lower confidence bound on recall (exact binomial) for a chosen number of score-0 cases and misses.

Implication. The gold set needs to be enriched with severe failures (found in production, produced by red-teaming, or written deliberately) rather than sampled only from typical traffic. Enriched sets overstate base rates, so recall is estimated on the enriched set and precision is estimated separately on the sampled one.


  
If the judge fails calibration

  
In order of preference: refine the judge prompt, add anchor examples, switch judge model, coarsen the scale (for example pass/fail per dimension), or use an ensemble with majority vote. Recalibrate after any judge prompt or model change, quarterly on a refreshed sample, and after any incident where the judge missed a real failure.







  
Testing and operating the evaluation

  
Test categories

  
Capability tests (offline)
Canonical happy paths for every intent in the taxonomy, two to ten examples each, with reference answers and expected tool calls. Sourced Added Agent behaviour varies between runs, so each case is run several times. Two measures answer different questions: pass@k is the chance that at least one of k attempts succeeds, and pass^k is the chance that all k succeed. The first suits tools where one success matters; the second suits customer-facing agents, where consistency is the requirement. The two diverge as k grows, so a customer-facing suite should report pass^k.
Suite size: published guidance suggests 20 to 50 simple tasks drawn from real failures is a good start. That is the size of a starting regression suite. The 200 to 500 trace gold set on the Calibration tab serves a different purpose, calibrating the judge.

  
Edge-case tests (offline)
Ambiguous requests, multi-turn dependencies, other languages if supported, typos and unusual phrasing, requests that span several intents.

  
Safety and scope tests (offline)
Out-of-scope requests (verify handoff or refusal), deny-list topics, probes for unauthorised actions, requests for information the agent must not reveal.

  
Adversarial tests (offline)
  • Prompt injection in user messages, attached documents or retrieved content.
  • Social engineering: claimed authority, urgency or sympathy to extract concessions.
  • Jailbreak attempts: role-play, hypotheticals, encodings.
  • Tool abuse: chaining tools in unintended ways, including permission escalation.
  • Memory and recommendation poisoning, where the agent keeps memory: hidden instructions that persistently bias later answers (for example toward certain products). Sourced Added Both memory poisoning and “AI recommendation poisoning” are described in Microsoft’s 2026 report.
  • Cross-agent interference, in multi-agent designs.
The corpus grows as attacks are observed and is rerun on every change. Sourced Added Results are reported as an attack success rate (successful attacks divided by attempted attacks) per category, which Microsoft’s automated red-teaming tooling uses as its primary metric. Its agent-specific categories (prohibited actions, sensitive-data leakage, task adherence, indirect prompt injection) match the failure types in this framework. The documentation gives no pass level, so any target is a Starting value set by the deployer.
Sourced A caution: the same company’s 2026 report says its research group found significant validity gaps in commonly used red-teaming metrics and LLM-as-judge evaluations, which can lead to unreliable conclusions about safety. Attack success rate is therefore useful as a trend on a fixed attack set, and should be reported with the attack set and the grader described, not read as an absolute safety measure.

  
Regression tests (offline)
Every confirmed production failure becomes a permanent test. Sourced Published guidance distinguishes capability evaluations, which start at low pass rates, from regression evaluations, which should stay near 100%, and describes mature capabilities graduating from one to the other.

  
Production sampling (online)
A continuous random sample of live traffic scored by the judge. It is the only set that reflects actual user behaviour, since offline suites drift from reality.

Release gates (CI/CD)

  
On every change listed under change management: run the full offline suite; block the deploy if any dimension’s average falls below its tier threshold, a zero-tolerance score appears, or a regression test fails; and require human approval if scores fall by more than a defined margin even within thresholds. Starting value The margin.


  
Production sampling and triage

  
Sample 1 to 5% of conversations daily (higher for new deployments, lower as confidence grows), stratified by intent so rare but important categories are covered. Starting value The sampling rate. Every score-0 or score-1 trace, and every null judgment, goes to a human review queue. Sourced Added A random sample of traces the judge passed is reviewed as well, because that is the only way to estimate how many failures the judge misses. This follows guidance from the FINOS AI governance framework, which treats the judge as a first-pass filter that makes human review more focused rather than replacing it. A flagged trace is classified (dimension, intent, prompt and model version), reviewed by a human, traced to a root cause (prompt, retrieval, model, tool or knowledge base), fixed, and added to the regression suite.

  
Sampling also bounds how rare a failure can be while going unseen. If n judged samples contain no severe failure, the 95% upper bound on the severe-failure rate is about 3/n.

  

    

    

  

  
The slider is logarithmic from 30 to 100,000 traces. This bound assumes random sampling and a judge that detects severe failures, which is exactly what calibration is meant to establish.


  
Reporting

  
Weekly to the product owner: score trends, top failure modes, regression-suite growth, calibration status. Monthly to leadership: deployment health and incidents. Quarterly to the governance committee: the full evaluation report, audit findings and framework version review.

Where the framework deliberately differs

  
Vendor rubric tooling combines dimension scores into one weighted number and compares it with a single pass level. This framework reports dimensions separately and applies a tail rule, because a composite can hide a severe failure in one dimension. Anyone using such tooling should export the per-dimension scores and apply the pass rules to those. The two approaches are not shown here to differ in outcome; the choice is a design judgment.


  
Known gaps

  
    
    
  • Fairness and inclusiveness. Version 0.4 adds a launch item for per-group quality of service, following the structure in Microsoft’s Standard: choose the groups (single factors and combinations, including languages), then set a minimum performance level and a maximum gap between groups. No numeric targets are proposed, because the Standard sets none and notes that trade-offs between group performance and overall performance are seldom clear, advising legal consultation. Inclusiveness, in the sense of accessibility testing of the agent and its disclosure, is still not covered. Microsoft’s Standard treats it as a single goal (I1) that points to a separate set of Microsoft accessibility standards, which were not available for this draft, so there is no requirement text to adopt. The 2026 report notes that one launch brought in accessibility and disability teams early. EU Article 50 separately asks for the AI disclosure to meet accessibility requirements.
    • 
    
  • Accessibility of the AI disclosure. Article 50 asks for the disclosure to be clear and to conform to accessibility requirements. The checklist item does not yet test accessibility.
    • 
    
  • Alignment. Anthropic states that reliable measures of whether an agent pursues goals the way users intend do not yet exist. The rubric measures outcomes and instruction compliance, not intent, so it should not be read as an alignment measure.
    • 
    
  • Management-system standard. ISO/IEC 42001 has not been mapped.

Evidence and limits

  
Known limitations

  
    
    
  • Judge ceiling. The judge is bounded by its own capability and biases. A well-calibrated judge still misses novel failure modes, so high-stakes cases need human review regardless of score.
    • 
    
  • Thresholds are not validated. The score thresholds, kappa and recall targets, sampling rates and cadences are starting values. They have not been tested against any deployment.
    • 
    
  • Rubric scope. Tone, empathy and cost are not scored.
    • 
    
  • Distribution shift. Calibration on past traffic does not guarantee accuracy on future traffic, so quarterly recalibration is a minimum.
    • 
    
  • Adversarial arms race. The adversarial suite is never complete.
    • 
    
  • Evaluation awareness. One vendor report describes models behaving differently when they detect evaluation. Offline suites may not predict production behaviour, so production sampling stays essential.
    • 
    
  • Vendor self-reports. The 2026 Transparency Report describes one vendor’s practices and results in its own words. It is evidence of what a large deployer does, not an independent measurement of outcomes. The Standard document cited is the General Requirements published on Microsoft’s site, labelled v2 and dated June 2022. The 2026 report says the Standard has since been re-engineered (core and scenario requirements, separate developer and deployer chapters), so the requirement numbers and some wording may differ from whatever internal version is now in force.
    • 
    
  • Bias studies are dated. The quantified judge biases come from 2023 to 2024 models.
    • 
    
  • Not legal advice. The regulatory points summarise public sources and do not establish what applies to a given deployment.

When to depart from the framework

  
The framework is a floor, not a ceiling. Depart toward more caution when entering a new market, language or regulatory regime; when the customer base includes vulnerable users; after an incident whose root cause is unconfirmed; and when a new model or tool is introduced and historical data no longer applies.

  
Sunset criteria

  
A deployment is sunset or reduced in scope if it cannot hold its tier thresholds after three consecutive improvement cycles, if customer satisfaction with AI-handled cases is materially worse than with human-handled cases over a sustained period, or if a regulatory change removes the basis for operating.
